Forgot to track your mileage?

It is tax time, you drove thousands of business miles, and there is no log. You cannot guess a number: the IRS says plainly that estimated amounts are not deductible. But you can rebuild the log trip by trip from records you already have, and a careful rebuild is worth real money: 10,000 business miles in 2026 are worth more than 7,000 dollars at the IRS rates. This guide shows how to do it.

What the IRS says about missing records

The rules are in chapter 5 of Publication 463:

  • No estimates. “You can't deduct amounts that you approximate or estimate.”
  • A record made at the time is the strongest. “A timely kept record has more value than a statement prepared later.”
  • Incomplete records can still be proven. If you don't have complete records, you prove each element with your own written or oral statement containing specific information, together with other supporting evidence.
  • Dates and places need documents. For the time and place of a trip, the supporting evidence must be direct or documentary, such as receipts, paid bills or statements from other people.
  • The purpose can follow from your work. Publication 463's own example: making deliveries is circumstantial evidence that the car was used for business, and the invoices of the deliveries establish when.

So a rebuilt log has to rest on documents, trip by trip. That is the whole method.

Rebuild the log in six steps

  1. Fix the total miles for the year. Find two odometer readings as close as possible to January 1 and December 31: service invoices, an inspection report, the purchase papers. The total is a required part of the record, and it puts a ceiling on everything else.
  2. List your working days. Go through the calendar and the invoices month by month and write down every day you drove for the business.
  3. Write one line per trip. Date, destination and business purpose, for example “Mar 12 – Hartwell Dental, 410 Oak St – install quote”.
  4. Add the miles from the addresses. Use the driving distance between the two addresses as a map shows it. That is a measured distance for a trip you can document, not a guess at a yearly total.
  5. Leave out the commute and private trips. Home to your regular place of work is commuting, however the day continued.
  6. Keep the evidence with the log. File the calendar pages, invoices and statements you used, and write on the log when you made it. A rebuilt log is honest about being rebuilt.

What sinks a rebuilt log

  • Round numbers. 50 miles every working day, or a neat 12,000 for the year.
  • A percentage. “About 70% of my driving is for work” is an estimate, and estimates are not deductible.
  • More business miles than the car drove. The odometer total from step 1 has to fit.
  • Trips without a purpose. Name the client, the job or the errand.

If you logged part of the year

Publication 463 allows sampling: a real log for part of the year can prove the business use for the whole year, if other evidence shows that those weeks were typical. The IRS example is a log kept during the first week of every month, with invoices and bills showing the same pattern in the other weeks. It works when your driving is the same every week. If it varies, rebuild the missing months trip by trip.

Stop it happening again

You only have to do this once. A log kept weekly counts as kept at the time, and an app that records every drive by itself makes even the weekly job a matter of swiping. Start today, not on January 1: the miles you drive this month count too, and at 76 cents each they add up quickly.

Common questions

Can I estimate my business mileage for the year?

No. Publication 463 says you can't deduct amounts that you approximate or estimate. Rebuild the trips one by one from records, or use a sample log that other evidence shows is typical for the year.

Does my phone's location history count as proof?

It is documentary evidence of where the phone was and when. It does not show why you drove, so add the client or the job to each trip, and keep the invoice or calendar entry that goes with it.

Can I rebuild the log from gas receipts?

Not from gas receipts alone. They show a date and a place, but not the trip or its purpose. Use them next to your calendar and invoices.

What if I never read the odometer?

Service invoices, inspection reports and the papers from buying or selling the car usually show the odometer with a date. Two of them, close to the start and the end of the year, give the total miles.

Can I still claim mileage for an earlier year?

You can generally amend a return within 3 years after the date you filed it, or within 2 years after the date you paid the tax, whichever is later. The same proof is needed for the earlier year.
