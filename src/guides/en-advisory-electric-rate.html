HMRC's advisory electric rate

The advisory electric rate (AER) is HMRC's figure for the electricity cost of a mile in a fully electric company car. Employers use it to reimburse business miles without tax, and it is the benchmark when an employee pays back the cost of private miles. Since September 2025 there are two rates, one for home charging and one for public charging. This guide gives the current figures, explains how the AER differs from the 55p approved mileage rate for your own car, and works through the common cases.

Updated 15 November 2026

The current rates

HMRC publishes the AER with its advisory fuel rates and reviews them every quarter, with new rates applying from 1 March, 1 June, 1 September and 1 December. You can keep using the previous rates for up to 1 month after a change.

The rates apply to fully electric cars only. Hybrids are treated as petrol or diesel cars and use the advisory fuel rate for their engine size.

How HMRC works out the rate

Both rates use the same efficiency, 3.59 miles per kWh, weighted by car sales. The home rate uses a domestic electricity price of 24.47p per kWh; the public rate uses 54.00p per kWh, the average price of slow and fast public chargers (under 50kW) from the Zapmap Price Index. Dividing price by efficiency gives the rounded 7p and 15p.

Your own cost per mile can be lower or higher. A car that is more efficient than average, or a cheaper home tariff, costs less than the rate; rapid chargers above 50kW are not in the public figure at all. HMRC lets employers pay more than the AER if they can show the electricity cost per mile is higher.

HMRC's page says the advisory rates only apply to employees using a company car and must not be used in any other circumstances. If you drive your own electric car for work, your employer can pay the approved mileage rate, the same as for a petrol car: see HMRC approved mileage rates 2026/27 and, for April 2027, HMRC mileage rates 2027/28.

Reimbursing business miles

If the employer pays no more than the AER for business miles in a company car, there is no taxable profit and no Class 1A National Insurance. Above it, unless the employer can show the cost per mile is higher, the excess is taxable and counts as earnings for Class 1 National Insurance.

When a car is charged both at home and on public chargers, HMRC allows the employer to apportion the business miles between the two rates according to where the charging happens, as long as the split is fair and reasonable.

Employers can also pay for the electricity directly. HMRC's manual (EIM23900) says charging a company car at a workplace charge point gives no taxable benefit, and neither does a charge card for public charge points. Reimbursing the cost of electricity used to charge the company car at home is exempt under section 239 ITEPA 2003, and disregarded for National Insurance (NIM06440), if the employer makes sure the payment is for the company car's electricity.

If your employer pays less than the cost, GOV.UK's page for employees says you can claim tax relief on what you spent on electricity for business trips in your company car, on the difference if part is reimbursed. Keep records of the actual cost.

Repaying private electricity

For petrol and diesel company cars, free private fuel triggers the car fuel benefit charge unless the employee records all private miles and pays them back at the advisory rate or more. Electricity is different: EIM23900 says electricity does not sit within the meaning of fuel, so the fuel benefit charge does not apply. The company car itself is still taxed as a benefit, but charging it at the employer's expense does not add a fuel charge.

So there is no tax reason for a company EV driver to repay private electricity. Many employers still ask for it in their car policy, for example when private trips are charged on a company card, and the AER is the rate HMRC publishes for that purpose.

Worked examples

All at the rates from 1 September 2026.

  • Home charging: Sam drives his electric company car 800 business miles in October and charges only at home. His employer pays 800 × 7p = £56, free of tax and National Insurance.
  • Home and public charging: the same 800 miles, but a quarter of the charging is done on public chargers. A fair split is 600 × 7p + 200 × 15p = £42 + £30 = £72.
  • Rapid chargers: on a long trip Sam pays 79p per kWh at a rapid charger. At 3.59 miles per kWh that is about 22p a mile, above the 15p rate. His employer can pay the higher figure for those miles if it keeps the evidence, such as receipts.
  • Paying too much: an employer pays 20p a mile for 800 home-charged business miles. The 13p above the AER, £104, is taxable and subject to Class 1 National Insurance unless the employer can show the higher cost.
  • Private miles on a company card: Sam drives 300 private miles charged on public chargers with the company's card. There is no fuel benefit charge. If his employer's policy asks him to pay the cost back, 300 × 15p = £45 at the AER.
  • Own electric car: Alex drives her own electric car 800 business miles. The AER doesn't apply; her employer can pay up to 800 × 55p = £440 tax-free.

What to record

  • Every business journey: date, start and destination, business reason and miles, as in any business mileage log
  • Private miles, if your employer asks for repayment or pays for all charging
  • Where the car was charged, home or public, so the miles can be split between the two rates
  • Receipts for public charging, especially rapid chargers, if you want more than 15p a mile
  • Which rate period each journey falls in, since the rates can change every quarter

Frequently asked questions

What is the advisory electric rate now?

From 1 September 2026, 7p a mile for home charging and 15p a mile for public charging. HMRC reviews it every quarter, so check GOV.UK for the rate on the date of your journey.

Can I use the AER for my own electric car?

No. The advisory rates are only for company cars. For your own car your employer can pay the approved mileage rate: 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles in 2026 to 2027, then 25p.

Does the AER apply to plug-in hybrids?

No. Hybrids are treated as petrol or diesel cars and use the advisory fuel rates for their engine size.

Is there a fuel benefit charge on an electric company car?

No. Electricity is not a fuel for the car fuel benefit charge. The car benefit charge on the car itself still applies.

Can my employer pay more than 15p for public charging?

Yes, if it can show the electricity cost per mile is higher, for example with receipts from rapid chargers. Otherwise the excess is taxable.
