Mileage log template for IRS records

The IRS has no official mileage log. Publication 463 lists what your records must prove and shows a sample log, marked “not an official Internal Revenue form”. This template turns those rules into columns you can use all year: what each column is for, a filled-in example, a CSV file to download, and how to turn the log into the numbers Schedule C asks for.

The columns

For car expenses, Publication 463 asks you to prove the date of each business use, the business destination, the business purpose and the mileage, plus the total miles for the year. The first five columns below cover that. The rest make the log easier to total and to explain.

ColumnWhy it is there
DateRequired: the date you used the car for business
StartWhere the trip began, so each trip can be followed
DestinationRequired: your business destination
Business purposeRequired: the client, job or errand
MilesRequired: the mileage of each business use
CategoryBusiness, commute, personal, medical or charity, so each total is a simple sum
Odometer start and endTies the miles to the car and gives you the total for the year
Parking and tollsBusiness parking fees and tolls are deductible on top of the standard mileage rate
VehicleNeeded if you drive more than one car; Schedule C asks for each vehicle separately

What to notice:

  • The drives between home and the studio are commuting. The studio is the designer's regular place of work, so those 22 miles are personal, even on a busy day. The trips from the studio to the client, the supplier and back are business: 54 miles, worth 39.15 dollars at the 72.5-cent rate that applied in March 2026.
  • Every purpose names the job. “Garcia job” says far more than “business” if anyone asks later.
  • The odometer runs without gaps. Each trip starts where the last one ended, so the log accounts for everything the car drove.
  • The toll goes in the log too. The road to the tile supplier cost a 2.10-dollar toll, recorded in the parking and tolls column. Business tolls are deducted on top of the mileage rate.
  • Medical miles have their own category. They are not business miles, but you can count them at the medical rate if you itemize medical expenses.

Totaling the year for Schedule C

If you claim the standard mileage rate and don't have to file Form 4562, you answer the vehicle questions in Part IV of Schedule C. Here is how the log feeds each line of the 2026 Schedule C:

Schedule C lineWhat it asksWhere it comes from in the log
43When you placed the vehicle in service for business (month/day/year)The date of the first business trip in this car
44aBusiness milesSum of the Business category for the year
44bCommuting milesSum of the Commute category
44cOther milesEverything else: personal, medical and charity
45Was the vehicle available for personal use during off-duty hours?Yes or no
46Do you (or your spouse) have another vehicle available for personal use?Yes or no
47aDo you have evidence to support your deduction?Yes, with a log like this one
47bIf “Yes,” is the evidence written?Yes: a written or computer log
9Car and truck expensesBusiness miles at the IRS rate, plus business parking fees and tolls

Lines 44a, 44b and 44c together should equal the miles the car drove in the year – the odometer on December 31 minus the odometer on January 1. If you used more than one vehicle, the Schedule C instructions ask you to attach a statement with the same information for each additional vehicle.

In 2026 the business rate changed in the middle of the year, so total the business miles separately before and after July 1. Suppose the designer's odometer read 16,610 on January 1 and 31,810 on December 31: 15,200 miles in total.

Paper, spreadsheet or app?

The IRS asks for an adequate record, not a particular format, and Publication 463 says a record prepared on a computer counts as adequate. Any of these can work:

OptionGood atWatch out for
Notebook in the carSimple, no battery, always at handAdding up by hand; easy to fall behind; one copy that can get lost
Spreadsheet (this CSV)Totals, categories and the July 1 split are easy to sum; easy to back upYou still type in every trip, address and odometer reading
App that records tripsDate, addresses and miles captured as you driveYou still add the purpose and check each trip; keep an export outside the phone

An app is not required. What matters is that the log is complete and kept as you go: Publication 463 counts a log kept weekly as timely, and gives much less weight to one rebuilt at the end of the year. For the full rules, see our guide to IRS mileage log requirements.

Common questions

Is there an official IRS mileage log template?

No. Publication 463 lists what your records must show and includes a sample daily mileage log, but marks it as not an official IRS form. You can use any layout that records the required details.

What columns does an IRS mileage log need?

The date, destination, business purpose and miles of each business trip, plus a way to show your total miles for the year. Odometer readings, a category column, parking and tolls, and the vehicle make the log easier to total.

Should personal trips go in the log?

You don't deduct them, but you need your total miles for the year. Logging personal and commuting trips in their own category, or reading the odometer at the start and end of the year, gives you that total for Schedule C line 44.

Can I use one log for two cars?

Yes, with a vehicle column, but total each car separately. Schedule C Part IV covers one vehicle, and the instructions ask for a statement with the same information for each additional vehicle.
