Passenger payments: the 5p rate

When you drive colleagues to a meeting, a site or a client, your employer can pay you an extra 5p a mile for each of them, free of tax and National Insurance. It's one of the smallest rates in HMRC's table and one of the most often misunderstood: it only covers fellow employees who are also on a business journey, and if your employer doesn't pay it you can't claim it from HMRC. This guide sets out the rules, with worked examples.

Updated 15 November 2026

The rate

HMRC's table of travel, mileage and fuel rates gives passenger payments as 5p per passenger per business mile for carrying fellow employees in a car or van. The rate is set in section 234 of the Income Tax (Earnings and Pensions) Act 2003 and has not changed in the 2026 or 2027 mileage changes: the car rate rose to 55p for the first 10,000 miles from 6 April 2026, and Budget 2026 announced 35p above 10,000 miles from 6 April 2027, but nothing was announced for passengers.

PaymentRate per business mile
Car or van, first 10,000 business miles, 2026 to 202755p
Car or van, each mile over 10,000, 2026 to 202725p
Each fellow employee carried on a business journey5p

The approved amount is worked out passenger by passenger. With two colleagues on board for the whole trip, every mile counts twice; HMRC's manual says the same mile may be counted more than once (EIM31405).

Who qualifies

Section 233 and HMRC's manual (EIM31400) set four conditions:

  • The driver is on a business journey in a car or van. Motorcycles and bicycles are not included.
  • The passengers are fellow employees. A client, a contractor or a family member is not.
  • The journey is business travel for each passenger too. Two colleagues going with you to the same off-site meeting qualify; a colleague you give a lift to the office does not, because for them it is ordinary commuting.
  • The payment is specifically for carrying passengers. A higher mileage rate paid whether or not you have passengers is not a passenger payment.

The vehicle can be your own or a company car or van. In your own car you need to be receiving mileage allowance payments for the journey; in a company car or van you need to be taxed on it as a benefit. The exemption doesn't apply to pool cars.

Tax and National Insurance

Passenger payments up to the approved amount are exempt from income tax. HMRC's employer guide, Business travel mileage: passenger payments, says the employer doesn't have to report them to HMRC or deduct National Insurance. For NICs the same approved amount is disregarded from earnings (NIM05870).

The test is made over the tax year: payments are approved to the extent that the year's total doesn't exceed the year's approved amount. Anything above it is not exempt.

No relief if your employer doesn't pay

This is the main difference from the car rate. If your employer pays less than 55p a mile for the car, you can claim Mileage Allowance Relief on the difference (see HMRC approved mileage rates 2026/27). Passenger payments have no such relief. EIM31410 says they only provide an exemption from tax: there is no relief or deduction if your employer pays nothing for passengers or less than 5p a mile per passenger. Whether you get anything for carrying colleagues is entirely up to your employer's expenses policy.

The self-employed have no passenger rate either. The simplified expenses flat rate is the same however many people are in the car.

Worked examples

Priya drives her own car 120 miles to a client and back, with two colleagues who are attending the same meeting. It's 2026 to 2027 and she is under 10,000 business miles for the year.

  • Employer pays 55p plus 5p per passenger: 120 × 55p = £66.00 for the car, and 120 × 5p × 2 = £12.00 for the passengers. All of it is tax-free and NICs-free.
  • One colleague is dropped at a site after 70 miles: the passenger miles are 120 + 70 = 190, so the approved passenger amount is 190 × 5p = £9.50.
  • Employer pays 8p per passenger mile: on the same 190 passenger miles that is £15.20. £9.50 is exempt; if her year has nothing else, the other £5.70 is taxable.
  • Employer pays 45p a mile and nothing for passengers: there's nothing to claim for the passengers. She can still claim Mileage Allowance Relief on the car: 120 × (55p − 45p) = £12.00 of unused approved amount.

HMRC's own examples (EIM31415) show the answers are the same when the driver uses a company car taxed as a benefit.

What to log

Your employer will want enough to check each claim, and HMRC can ask how a payment was worked out. For each journey with passengers, keep:

  • the date, start and destination, and the business reason for the trip
  • the business miles driven
  • the name of each passenger and the miles they were carried, if they joined or left on the way
  • why the journey was business travel for them, for example the same meeting or site visit

The trip details are the same as for any business mileage claim; What HMRC needs in a business mileage log covers them.

Frequently asked questions

Is the passenger rate 5p per passenger or 5p per trip?

Per passenger per business mile. Two colleagues carried for 100 miles give an approved amount of 2 × 100 × 5p = £10.

Can I claim 5p from HMRC if my employer doesn't pay it?

No. Passenger payments are an exemption only. Unlike the car rate, there is no relief for an unpaid or underpaid passenger amount.

Does it apply if I drive a company car?

Yes, if you're taxed on the car as a benefit and the other conditions are met. It doesn't apply to pool cars.

Does a colleague I drive to the office count?

No. The journey must be business travel for the passenger as well. A lift to their normal workplace is commuting for them.

Has the 5p rate gone up with the 55p car rate?

No. The car rate changed from 6 April 2026 and the rate above 10,000 miles is due to change from 6 April 2027, but the passenger rate is still 5p.
