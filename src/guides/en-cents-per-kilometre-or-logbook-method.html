Cents per kilometre or logbook method?

The ATO gives you two ways to claim a car you own or lease and drive for work. Cents per kilometre is a set rate, 91 cents in 2026–27, for at most 5,000 km per car. The logbook method is your real costs multiplied by your work-use percentage, with no kilometre limit. Which one gives more depends on two numbers: how far you drive for work and what the car costs you. This guide puts both through the same figures.

You are not locked in. The ATO lets you use a different method for each car, and change method from one income year to the next for the same car. So the question can be asked again every year, as long as you have the records for both.

Example 1: a few thousand kilometres a year

Priya is a sole trader. In 2026–27 her car travels 12,000 km, of which 3,000 km are for work. Her 12-week logbook shows 25% work use. The car costs her:

Expense2026–27
Fuel$1,900
Registration$850
Insurance$1,150
Servicing and tyres$700
Decline in value$2,500
Total$7,100
  • Cents per kilometre: 3,000 × 91 cents = $2,730
  • Logbook method: 7,100 × 25% = $1,775

Cents per kilometre gives $955 more, and Priya needs no receipts for it.

Example 2: on the road most days

Tom is a sole trader who visits customers across the state. His car travels 24,000 km in 2026–27, 18,000 km of it for work. His logbook shows 75% work use. The car costs him:

Expense2026–27
Fuel$3,900
Registration$900
Insurance$1,400
Servicing$1,100
Tyres$600
Loan interest$1,300
Decline in value$5,000
Total$14,200
  • Cents per kilometre: limited to 5,000 km, so 5,000 × 91 cents = $4,550
  • Logbook method: 14,200 × 75% = $10,650

The logbook method gives $6,100 more. Thirteen thousand of Tom's work kilometres earn nothing under cents per kilometre; under the logbook method every one of them counts. The figures in both examples are made up; your own costs and logbook decide the result.

A quick way to see where you stand

  • Under 5,000 work km: cents per kilometre gives 91 cents for each work kilometre. The logbook method beats that only if your whole-year costs, divided by all the kilometres the car travels, come to more than 91 cents a kilometre. Priya's car costs 7,100 ÷ 12,000 = 59 cents a kilometre, so cents per kilometre wins.
  • Over 5,000 work km: cents per kilometre stays at $4,550 however far you drive. The logbook method keeps growing with your costs and your percentage. The further past 5,000 km you are, the more likely the logbook wins.
  • An expensive or new car: decline in value is often the largest single cost, and only the logbook method lets you claim it as such.

Decline in value in brief

Under the logbook method the car's decline in value is a car expense. The ATO's determination of a car's effective life is generally 8 years. With the diminishing value method that is 25% of the remaining value a year; with the prime cost method, 12.5% of the cost a year. There is a limit on the cost you can use, the car limit: $69,674 for cars bought in 2025–26. The ATO's own example: a car bought for $30,000 on 1 July, prime cost method, gives $3,750 for the year; at 11% work use the claim is $413. Under cents per kilometre you can't claim decline in value separately, because the rate already includes it.

Employees: the $1,000 standard deduction

From 2026–27 employees have a standard deduction of $1,000 for work-related expenses, and car expense claims count against it. For an employee, work-related claims start to add something once together they pass $1,000. If the car is your only claim, at 91 cents that is from about 1,100 work kilometres a year. Income you earn as a sole trader is outside the standard deduction, so Priya's and Tom's figures above stand as they are.

Keep the records for both

You can only choose the better method at tax time if both are open to you. Cents per kilometre asks for a record of your work kilometres. The logbook method asks for a valid 12-week logbook, the odometer readings at the start and end of each year, and the receipts. A trip log kept all year, with one 12-week logbook period inside it and the receipts in one place, covers both. The ATO 12-week logbook lists what the logbook must contain, and ATO cents per kilometre 2026–27 covers the rate and its limit.

Frequently asked questions

Can I switch methods from year to year?

Yes. The ATO says you can change the method you use in different income years for the same car, and use a different method for each car.

Can I use the logbook method without a logbook?

No. Without a valid logbook the method isn't available; cents per kilometre may be.

Is there a kilometre limit on the logbook method?

No. The claim is your car expenses multiplied by your work-related use percentage, whatever the distance.

What is the most I can claim with cents per kilometre?

5,000 km per car: $4,550 at the 2026–27 rate of 91 cents.
