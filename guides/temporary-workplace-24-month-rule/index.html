The temporary workplace 24-month rule

Whether the drive to a client's office, a project site or a second branch is business mileage depends on one question: is it a temporary workplace or a permanent one? HMRC answers it with the 24-month rule and the 40% test in section 339 of the Income Tax (Earnings and Pensions) Act 2003. This guide sets out the rule from the legislation and HMRC's guidance, what happens when a posting is extended or cut short, how fixed-term jobs are treated, and worked examples with the approved rate.

Updated 15 November 2026

Why the workplace decides the mileage

Section 338 of ITEPA 2003 gives employees a deduction for travel they have to make to attend a place in the performance of their duties, but not for ordinary commuting: travel between home and a permanent workplace. Travel between home and a temporary workplace is not commuting, so it can be business travel. The same line decides what an employer can pay tax-free: approved mileage allowance payments cover business travel only, which section 236 defines by reference to the journeys that sections 337 to 342 allow.

Section 339(3) defines a temporary workplace as a place you attend in the performance of your duties for the purpose of a task of limited duration or for some other temporary purpose. A permanent workplace is one you attend regularly that is not temporary (section 339(2)). A place that is the base from which your duties are performed, or where your tasks are allocated, is permanent if you attend it regularly (section 339(4)), which is why a depot you report to each morning is a permanent workplace.

The 24-month rule and the 40% test

Section 339(5) stops a place being a temporary workplace if you attend it in the course of a period of continuous work that lasts, or is reasonably expected to last, more than 24 months. Section 339(6) says a period is continuous work at a place if your duties are performed there "to a significant extent", and HMRC's booklet 490 (section 3.19) and manual (EIM32080) take that to mean 40% or more of your working time.

Both parts must be met for the place to become permanent (490, section 3.22):

Expected time at the siteShare of working time thereResult
24 months or lessAny share, even full timeTemporary – travel can be business mileage
More than 24 monthsUnder 40%Temporary – travel can be business mileage
More than 24 months40% or morePermanent – the journey is ordinary commuting

The rule only works one way. EIM32080 explains that it turns places that would otherwise be temporary into permanent ones; it never makes a permanent workplace temporary. A place you attend for a job with no end in sight is permanent even if you are only there 20% of the time, because the visits are not for a limited-duration task or a temporary purpose. And a break in attendance does not necessarily end a period of continuous work (490, section 3.25).

Expectations count, not hindsight

The test looks at what it is reasonable to expect at the time of each journey (section 339(5)(b)). HMRC's starting point is what the employer has told the employee about the length of the assignment (EIM32100); whether you moved house is a factor, not a test. That gives three cases:

  • You know from the start it will be longer than 24 months. The site is permanent from day one, even if the job later ends early. In 490's example 13, Hassan is posted full time for 28 months; it ends after 18, and he gets no relief at any point.
  • A short posting is extended. The site is temporary until the expectation changes and permanent from then on. Richard (example 14) is sent for 18 months, extended to 28 after 10: relief for the first 10 months only.
  • A long posting is cut short. The reverse: Sarah (example 15) is sent for 28 months, shortened to 18 after 10, and gets relief for the final 8 months.

In practice that means keeping the evidence of what you were told – the email, letter or project plan with the expected end date – with your mileage log, and noting the date when an extension is agreed.

Fixed-term jobs

Section 339(5)(a)(ii) adds a second limb: a place is not temporary if your continuous work there makes up all or almost all of the period you are likely to hold the employment. A short contract therefore does not make the site temporary. HMRC treats "almost all" as more than 80% of the likely length of the employment (490, section 3.28; EIM32125). In example 26, Mike is taken on for an 18-month fixed term to work at one site and gets no relief for travel to it, although it is well under 24 months.

Worked examples

Priya is employed at an office in Reading. Her employer sends her to a client in Basingstoke three days a week for a project expected to last 18 months; the round trip from home is 56 miles. Three days out of five is 60% of her working time, but the project is not expected to last more than 24 months, so Basingstoke is a temporary workplace – like Neil in 490's example 19, who spends three days a week in Bradford for 18 months.

  • In the first 40 weeks, all in the 2026 to 2027 tax year, she makes 120 trips: 120 × 56 = 6,720 business miles.
  • Approved amount at 55p for the first 10,000 business miles: 6,720 × 55p = £3,696. Her employer can pay up to that tax-free; if it pays less, she can claim Mileage Allowance Relief on the difference.
  • After those 40 weeks the client extends the project to 30 months. From that date Priya expects to spend more than 40% of her time there for more than 24 months, so Basingstoke is a permanent workplace and the trips are ordinary commuting. Mileage paid for them after that date is taxable.

Tom, based in Portsmouth, works at a Southampton site one and a half days a week for 28 months. That is 30% of his time, under 40%, so the site stays temporary for the whole period, as it does for Edward in example 16. Caroline (example 17) is the opposite case: four days a week in Swansea for an expected 30 months makes Swansea permanent, and her one day a week at her old Cardiff base stays permanent too.

For 2027 to 2028 the rate above 10,000 business miles is due to rise to 35p from 6 April 2027, as announced at Budget 2026; see HMRC approved mileage rates 2027/28.

When a temporary trip is still commuting

Even a journey to a genuinely temporary workplace counts as ordinary commuting if it is in practice substantially the same as your normal commute: broadly the same direction and a similar length (section 338(2); EIM32300). HMRC's manual tells its officers not to argue this where the extra distance is 10 miles or more each way. In EIM32306 an employee whose commute is 9 miles drives to a factory 11 miles beyond her office, 20 miles in all, and gets relief for the whole journey. A site in a completely different direction is never caught. The wider rules – calling at the office on the way, working from home – are in Commuting vs business mileage.

Does the rule apply to the self-employed?

No. Sections 338 and 339 are employment income rules. For the self-employed, travel costs must be incurred wholly and exclusively for the trade, and HMRC's business manual decides home-to-site travel on the facts: a trader whose home is the base of an itinerant trade, such as a builder moving between short jobs, can claim the travel (BIM37620), but there is no 24-month or 40% test. Workers who work through their own limited company should also read section 339A, which can treat each engagement as a separate employment.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 24 months counted from my first visit?

It is counted over the period of continuous work at the site, judged by what you reasonably expect. If you expect from the start to be there more than 24 months for 40% or more of your time, the site is permanent from the first day.

What if my posting is extended past 24 months?

The site stays temporary up to the date the extension is agreed, so earlier trips remain business mileage. From that date it is a permanent workplace, if you spend 40% or more of your time there, and the journey is ordinary commuting.

Does a break restart the 24 months?

Not necessarily. HMRC says a break in attendance does not by itself end a period of continuous work; in 490's example 21, a three-month gap did not reset the count.

I am at a site two days a week. Does the 40% test apply?

Two days of a five-day week is 40% of your working time, and HMRC's test is 40% or more. If that pattern is expected to last more than 24 months, the site becomes a permanent workplace.

Can a contract of under 24 months still be a permanent workplace?

Yes, if the job itself is that short. Under the fixed-term limb, a site where you spend more than 80% of the likely length of the employment is not temporary.
