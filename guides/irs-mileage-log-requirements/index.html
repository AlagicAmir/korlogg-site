IRS mileage log requirements

If you deduct car expenses or are reimbursed for business miles, you must be able to prove them. The IRS does not prescribe a form, but Publication 463 says exactly what your records need to show: the date, the miles, the destination and the business purpose of each trip, plus your total miles for the year. This guide goes through those rules, who they apply to, and the mistakes that cost people their deduction.

What a mileage log must show

Chapter 5 of Publication 463 lists the elements you must be able to prove for car expenses. Put into the columns of a log, that means:

RecordWhat the IRS asks for
DateThe date you used the car for business
DestinationYour business destination – the place you drove to
Business purposeWhy the trip was for business, such as the client you met or the job you went to
Business milesThe mileage for each business use of the car
Total miles for the yearAll miles the car was driven in the tax year, business and personal
Parking fees and tollsThe cost of each one, if you deduct them on top of the standard mileage rate
Car cost and first business useIf you deduct actual expenses: the cost of the car and any improvements, and the date you started using it for business

The total miles for the year matter because your deduction depends on the share of the car's use that was for business. Schedule C also asks for them: line 44 splits the miles you drove during the year into business, commuting and other. Reading the odometer on January 1 and December 31 is the simplest way to get that total. Publication 463's sample log (Table 5-2) also has columns for the odometer at the start and end of each trip.

What counts as an adequate record

Publication 463 says you should keep your proof in “an account book, diary, log, statement of expense, trip sheets, or similar record”. A few points from the same chapter decide whether a log holds up:

  • It must be written. Written evidence is more reliable than oral evidence alone, so the IRS generally expects a written record. A record kept on a computer counts as an adequate record.
  • It must be made at or near the time. The IRS calls this a timely kept record: one written when you drive has more value than a statement prepared later. You don't have to write every trip down the same day; a log kept weekly that accounts for the week's use is considered timely.
  • Estimates don't count. In its own words, “You can't deduct amounts that you approximate or estimate.”
  • The purpose must be clear. You generally need a written statement of the business purpose. If it is obvious from the circumstances, you may not have to write it out (see the sales route below).

Regular routes

Publication 463 gives the example of a sales representative who calls on customers along an established route. They don't have to explain the business purpose of each trip. It is enough to record the length of the route once, the date of each trip at or near the time, and the total miles driven during the year.

Sampling: a log for part of the year

You may keep an adequate record for only part of the year and use it to prove your business use for the whole year, as long as you can show with other evidence that the period you logged is representative. The IRS example: a log kept during the first week of every month shows 75% business use, and invoices and bills show the same pattern in the other weeks. Sampling works best when your driving is the same every week. If it varies, a full log is much easier to defend.

Who needs a mileage log

  • Self-employed people who file Schedule C, including 1099 contractors and gig drivers. Whether you use the standard mileage rate or actual expenses, you report car expenses on line 9 and answer the vehicle questions in Part IV (or on Form 4562 if you depreciate the car). They include “Do you have evidence to support your deduction?” and “If ‘Yes,’ is the evidence written?” (lines 47a and 47b).
  • Employees reimbursed under an accountable plan. To keep a reimbursement out of your wages, you must adequately account to your employer: give them a record of each trip made at or near the time, within a reasonable period. Publication 463 treats accounting within 60 days after the expense as reasonable. With a mileage allowance at or below the IRS rate, you still prove the time (dates), place and business purpose of each trip.
  • Anyone with two kinds of work. If you are self-employed and also an employee, Publication 463 says to keep separate records for each business activity.

Employees who are not reimbursed generally can't deduct their car expenses on their federal return. Form 2106 is now limited to Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis state or local government officials and employees with impairment-related work expenses.

Commuting is not business mileage

Driving between your home and your main or regular place of work is personal commuting, however far it is and even if you take business calls on the way. Leave it out of your business miles; on Schedule C it goes on line 44b. Publication 463 lists the main exceptions:

  • You have a regular work location away from home and drive to a temporary work location in the same trade or business. A location is generally temporary if the work there is realistically expected to last, and does last, one year or less.
  • You drive to a temporary work site outside the metropolitan area where you live and normally work.
  • Your home is your principal place of business, and you drive from home to another work location in the same business.

Trips from one client or work site to another during the day are business miles.

What happens without a log

If your return is examined and you can't support your mileage, the deduction can be disallowed. Publication 463 says that if your records are incomplete, you must prove each missing element with your own written or oral statement containing specific information, together with other supporting evidence. For the date and place of a trip, that evidence has to be direct or documentary, such as invoices or delivery records. It is a much harder position than handing over a log.

For employees, mileage reimbursements that don't meet the accountable plan rules are treated as paid under a nonaccountable plan, which means your employer reports them as wages on your Form W-2.

Common mistakes

  • Rebuilding the year in December. A log written months later from a calendar is exactly the “statement prepared later” the IRS gives less weight to. Keep it as you go, at least weekly.
  • Counting the commute. Home to your regular workplace and back is never business mileage.
  • No total for the year. Without the total miles you can't show the business share or fill in line 44 of Schedule C. Note the odometer on January 1.
  • A purpose of “work” or “business”. Name the client, the job or the errand.
  • One rate for all of 2026. The business rate is 72.5 cents a mile through June 30, 2026, and 76 cents from July 1. Total your business miles for each half of the year separately; our IRS mileage deduction calculator does the arithmetic.

How long to keep the log

Keep the records as long as they may be needed for the tax law. Publication 463 says that generally means 3 years from the date you file the return on which you claim the deduction; a return filed early counts as filed on the due date. Some cases are longer: Publication 583 gives 6 years if you leave out income worth more than 25% of the gross income shown on your return. If you depreciate the car, keep the business-use records for each year of the car's recovery period.

Common questions

Does the IRS have an official mileage log form?

No. Publication 463 lists what your records must show and includes a sample daily mileage log, marked as not an official IRS form. A notebook, a spreadsheet or an app can all work if the log has the required details.

Is a mileage log on my phone or computer acceptable?

Yes. Publication 463 says a record prepared on a computer is considered an adequate record. It still needs the date, destination, business purpose and miles of each trip, and it should be kept at or near the time of the trips.

Can I write up my mileage log at the end of the year?

The IRS gives far more weight to a record made at or near the time of each trip. A weekly log counts as timely; a log reconstructed months later from memory is weak evidence, and estimated amounts can't be deducted.

Do I need odometer readings for every trip?

Publication 463 asks for the miles of each business use and the total miles for the year, not for an odometer reading on each trip. Its sample log has odometer columns, and readings at the start and end of the year are the easiest way to prove the total.

How long should I keep my mileage log?

Generally 3 years from the date you file the return that claims the deduction, longer in some cases, such as 6 years if you underreport income by more than 25%. Keep the records for each year of the recovery period if you depreciate the car.
