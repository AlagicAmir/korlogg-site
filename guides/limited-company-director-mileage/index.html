Limited company director mileage

If you run your own limited company and drive for it, there are two ways to do it: use your own car and have the company pay you a mileage rate, or have the company provide a car. They are taxed very differently. This guide explains how a director claims mileage from the company at HMRC's approved rate, what changes with a company car, the records the company needs, the VAT the company may reclaim on the fuel element, and where IR35 changes the picture.

Updated 15 November 2026

A director counts as an employee here

The mileage rules in the Income Tax (Earnings and Pensions) Act 2003 are written for employments, and section 5 applies them equally to offices: the director is the "employee" and the company is the "employer". So the approved mileage allowance payments, the business travel rules and Mileage Allowance Relief work for you exactly as they do for any employee. GOV.UK adds one practical condition: if the company is to pay you a salary, expenses or benefits, you must register the company as an employer.

Option 1: your own car, mileage paid by the company

When you drive your own car on company business, the company can pay you up to the approved amount without tax, National Insurance or any report to HMRC. For cars and vans the rates per business mile are:

Tax yearFirst 10,000 business milesEach mile over 10,000
2026 to 2027 (from 6 April 2026)55p25p
2027 to 2028 (from 6 April 2027, announced)55p35p
2011 to 2012 up to 2025 to 202645p25p

The 35p rate was announced at Budget 2026 and is to be set by statutory instrument; details are in HMRC approved mileage rates 2027/28. The 10,000 miles count all your own vehicles together and start again on 6 April.

  • 6,000 business miles in 2026 to 2027: the company can pay 6,000 × 55p = £3,300, tax-free and with nothing to report.
  • 14,000 business miles in 2026 to 2027: 10,000 × 55p + 4,000 × 25p = £6,500. If the company pays 55p for every mile (£7,700), the extra £1,200 is taxable: it either goes through payroll or onto a P11D. For National Insurance the qualifying amount is 55p for every business mile with no 10,000-mile step, so in this example no Class 1 NICs are due on the £1,200.

GOV.UK says the rate covers the cost of owning and running the vehicle, so fuel, vehicle tax, MOTs and repairs cannot be claimed on top. For the company, the payments are a business cost: expenses incurred wholly and exclusively for the trade are deductible when its profits are worked out (BIM37600, section 54 of the Corporation Tax Act 2009).

If the company pays less than the approved amount, or nothing, you can claim Mileage Allowance Relief on the difference: in your Self Assessment return if you send one, otherwise online or by post. Relief only gives back tax at your rate – 20% of the shortfall for a basic-rate taxpayer – and GOV.UK says it cannot be more than the tax you paid in that year. Having the company pay the approved rate puts the full amount in your pocket instead.

No simplified expenses for a company

GOV.UK is explicit that simplified expenses cannot be used by limited companies or by partnerships with a company as a partner. The 55p figure a sole trader can deduct as a flat rate is not available to the company for its own vehicles. What the company can do is pay you, as its employee, the approved mileage rate for your own car – the same pence per mile, but under the employment rules above, not as a business deduction method.

Option 2: a company car

A car the company owns or leases and lets you use privately is a benefit in kind. GOV.UK's employer guidance says the company must report the private use, file a P11D at the end of the tax year and pay Class 1A National Insurance on the car benefit, worked out from the car's list price. If the company also pays for fuel you use privately and you do not pay it back in the same tax year, the car fuel benefit applies as well, again with a P11D and Class 1A.

  • Business-only cars are exempt only if private use is actually prohibited and checked; a car simply mostly used for work is not.
  • Business mileage in a company car earns no approved mileage rate. If you pay for the fuel and the company reimburses business miles, HMRC's advisory fuel rates are the figures to use; if the company pays all the fuel, you repay the private miles at those rates to avoid the fuel benefit.
  • From 6 April 2027 company cars and car fuel must be payrolled: reported through payroll on the Full Payment Submission, with no P11D or P11D(b) for those benefits. Budget 2026 confirmed the date.

Either way you need the log. With a company car it proves the private miles you repaid; with your own car it supports every pound the company paid you.

Records the company needs

The mileage payments are company money, so the company needs evidence behind them. For each business journey keep the date, the start and end point (GOV.UK asks for postcodes), the business reason and the miles, plus the claim you made to the company and its payment. The company must keep accounting records, including records of all money spent, for 6 years from the end of the last company financial year they relate to; payroll records, including taxable expenses and benefits, for 3 years from the end of the tax year. What a good log contains is in What HMRC needs in a business mileage log.

VAT on the fuel element

If the company is VAT-registered, it can reclaim VAT on the fuel part of the mileage it pays you. HMRC's VAT Notice 700/64 sets the conditions:

  • The allowance must be based on mileage actually driven (section 9.8), and the input tax is the fuel element multiplied by the VAT fraction: 20 ÷ 120, one sixth at the standard rate.
  • The fuel element can be worked out with HMRC's advisory fuel rates; the notice also accepts rates from recognised motoring organisations (section 9.10).
  • The company must keep VAT invoices for the fuel, covering the business fuel, and an invoice dated after the period of the claim cannot support it (section 9.10).
  • Records per person: miles driven, business or private, the engine size, the mileage rate and the VAT claimed (section 9.9).

Example: 6,000 business miles in a petrol car of 1,401 to 2,000cc. At the advisory fuel rate from 1 September 2026, 17p a mile, the fuel element is £1,020 and the VAT is £1,020 ÷ 6 = £170. HMRC reviews the advisory rates every quarter (1 March, 1 June, 1 September, 1 December), so use the rate for the date of the journeys. Notice 700/64 is written in terms of employees and does not mention directors separately; check the point with your accountant if you are unsure.

Where you drive: temporary workplaces and IR35

The mileage only qualifies for journeys that count as business travel. A trip from home to a client's premises is business travel if the client's site is a temporary workplace for you; it stops being one if you expect to work there more than 24 months for 40% or more of your time – see The temporary workplace 24-month rule. Working from home does not by itself make home a workplace.

Contractors should check one more rule. Under section 339A of ITEPA 2003, where you provide your services to a client through an intermediary, each engagement can be treated as a separate employment for travel, which makes the client's site a permanent workplace and the journey there ordinary commuting. HMRC's guidance on employment intermediaries says that for workers using their own company this restriction applies where the IR35 rules apply to the engagement, and not where the company is outside IR35; section 339A has matching provisions for the off-payroll rules. If your contract is inside IR35, assume home-to-client mileage is not tax-free.

Frequently asked questions

Does the company have to report my mileage payments on a P11D?

Not if they are within the approved amount for your own car. Only the part above the approved amount has to be taxed through payroll or reported on a P11D. A company car is reported separately, and from 6 April 2027 it goes through payroll instead of a P11D.

Can I claim 55p a mile and the fuel as well?

No. GOV.UK says the approved rate covers the cost of owning and running your vehicle; fuel, vehicle tax, MOTs and repairs cannot be claimed separately.

Can my company use simplified expenses for my car?

No. GOV.UK says simplified expenses cannot be used by limited companies. The company can pay you the approved mileage rate as its employee, which is a different route to a similar figure per mile.

Does it make a difference that my car is electric?

Not for the approved mileage rate: HMRC's table has one rate for cars and vans whatever the fuel. The VAT rules in Notice 700/64 summarised above are written for road fuel.

Can the company pay me mileage if I take no salary?

Yes, but GOV.UK says the company must be registered as an employer to pay you a salary, expenses or benefits.
