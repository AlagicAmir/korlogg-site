ATO cents per kilometre 2026–27

If you drive your own car for work, the cents per kilometre method is the simple way to claim it: a set rate for each work-related kilometre, up to 5,000 km per car, with no receipts. For the 2026–27 income year the rate is 91 cents. This guide covers what the rate includes, where the limit sits, the record the ATO expects and what the claim comes to.

Updated 19 November 2026

The rate, year by year

Income year (1 July – 30 June)Rate per kilometreMost you can claim per car (5,000 km)

2024–2588 cents$4,400
2023–2485 cents$4,250
2022–2378 cents$3,900

Use the rate of the income year you are claiming for. A trip on 30 June 2026 is claimed at 88 cents, a trip on 1 July 2026 at 91 cents.

How the method works

  • Multiply your work-related kilometres by the rate. 2,400 km in 2026–27 is 2,400 × 91 cents = $2,184.
  • At most 5,000 work-related kilometres per car per year. Kilometres above that earn nothing under this method.
  • The rate covers everything. In the ATO's words it covers all of your car expenses, including decline in value, registration and insurance, maintenance, repairs and fuel. You can't add these, or any other car expense, on top.
  • No receipts. You don't need fuel or service receipts for this method.
  • You must own or lease the car. A car under a salary sacrifice or novated lease arrangement doesn't count as yours for this purpose.

Two cars are two limits: you can claim up to 5,000 km for each. If you and a joint owner both use the car for separate income-producing purposes, each of you can claim up to 5,000 km. Parking and tolls are not car expenses, so they are not part of the rate.

Which kilometres count

Work-related kilometres are the ones the car travels in the course of earning your income: from one workplace to another, to clients, to a supplier, to perform your duties. Driving between home and your regular place of work is private, with a few exceptions the ATO lists on its page about trips you can and can't claim. If a trip is partly private, only the work part counts.

The record the ATO expects

No receipts doesn't mean no record. The ATO says you need to be able to show two things: that you own the car, and how you worked out your work-related kilometres. It gives a diary as an example of such a record.

The kilometres may be a reasonable estimate. The ATO's own example: a 20 km work-related round trip once a week over 48 working weeks is 960 km. An estimate built on a pattern like that needs the pattern to be written down: which trip, how long it is, how often you made it. A log of the actual trips answers the same question with no estimating at all, and it also tells you the day you pass 5,000 km.

Worked examples for 2026–27

  • A few client visits a week. Mia drives 26 km there and back to a client twice a week for 46 weeks: 2 × 26 × 46 = 2,392 km. Her claim is 2,392 × 91 cents = $2,176.72.
  • Just over the limit. Daniel's trips add up to 5,700 work-related kilometres. He can claim 5,000 × 91 cents = $4,550. The other 700 km are not claimed under this method.
  • Two cars. Sara drives 3,100 work-related kilometres in one car and 1,400 in another. Each car has its own limit, so she claims 4,500 × 91 cents = $4,095.

When your work driving runs well past 5,000 km, or the car is costly to run, the logbook method can give a larger claim, because it has no kilometre limit. Cents per kilometre or logbook method compares the two with figures, and The ATO 12-week logbook explains what that logbook must contain. You can use a different method for each car, and change method from one income year to the next for the same car.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cents per kilometre rate for 2026–27?

91 cents per kilometre, for the income year from 1 July 2026 to 30 June 2027.

Can I claim fuel on top of the rate?

No. The rate already covers fuel, registration, insurance, servicing, repairs and the car's decline in value.

Do I need a logbook for cents per kilometre?

You don't need the 12-week logbook; that belongs to the logbook method. You do need a record that shows how you worked out your work-related kilometres.

What if I drive more than 5,000 km for work?

The claim under this method stops at 5,000 km per car. Compare it with the logbook method, which uses your actual costs and your work-use percentage.
